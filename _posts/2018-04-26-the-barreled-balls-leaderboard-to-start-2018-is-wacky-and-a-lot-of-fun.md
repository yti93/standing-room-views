One of the newest additions to Statcast's many measurements is Barreled Balls. It's a fun way to describe how many hitters catch the ball with the fat part of their bat. Usually, that translates to hitting the ball both very fast and very far - a barreled ball is defined by a high probability batting average and slugging percentage well above MVP-level production (.500 BA, 1.500 slugging). It's April 25th, so admittedly the leaderboard could look very different in a couple months, but let's have some fun, shall we? (All players discussed below meet the minimum Batted Ball Events requirement of 25).

Statcast measures Barrels in terms of raw stats (Barrels) and rate stats (Barrels per Batted Ball Event and Barrels per Plate Appearance). (An aside - a batted ball event [BBE] is any at-bat that results in a ball being put in play. This becomes the lowest common denominator in Statcast calculations so that hitter samples can be compared against one another. If you want to read more on Statcast metrics, I highly recommend checking out their glossary here.

After last night's 2 HR performance, Mookie Betts is now the Major League leader in Barreled balls with 16. The rest of the top 10 looks as you would expect, with big sluggers such as Joey Gallo, Aaron Judge, and Mike Trout peppering the top 10 with their juicy swings.

When you flip the switch and start looking at barreled balls as a proportion of total Batted Ball Events, your leader is Teoscar Hernandez, who barrels the ball up almost 30% of the time his at-bat ends in a batted ball event. Granted, he's got almost 50% less BBEs than J.D. Martinez in 2nd place, but that's still an absurd barreled ball rate. This list is more skewed by sample size than anything else, where the top 10 "Barrelers/BBE" have as little as 26 BBEs (Scott Schebler, 5th with a 23.1% Brls/BBE) to 70 BBEs (Mookie Betts, 7th with 22.9% Brls/BBE).

Lastly, Teoscar Hernandez again tops the leaderboard with a 19.6% Brls/PA rate, just ahead of Scott Schebler's 19.4% rate. Betts, Martinez, and Gallo also make appearances in the top 10, rounding out an eclectic mix of in-their-prime sluggers and relative newcomers riding red-hot starts.

What's the takeaway behind all this? Hitting the ball hard produces better results on balls in play. Look no further then Hernandez's start - hitting over .300 and slugging over .600 to improve on his performance last year in limited plate appearances. The Blue Jays have decided he deserves a longer look in their lineup, and at 25 years of age, he deserves more than deserves that audition.

"But Youssef", you say, "Isn't that common sense that hitting the ball harder will produce better results"?

To that I say, yes. What Statcast is doing with these results is putting numbers to theories, proving conjectures correct and inviting approach changes throughout major league baseball. Before, Teoscar Hernandez would have been just a hot April start dismissed. Looking into the underlying metrics, he hits the ball exceptionally hard at an exceptional rate and he also leads the league in average exit velocity, which means he has a knack for putting the fat part of the barrel on the ball. In a league where good contact is becoming a premium skill, the ability to hit the ball hard and far consistently won him an everyday spot in the lineup. I'd say that's a benefit to proving common sense theories correct with the data.
