Some eight months after pitchers and catchers reported to sunny climes in Arizona and Florida, we have arrived at probably the best two-week stretch in sports all year. Game 1 of the World Series coincides with NBA opening night, just a couple of slapshots removed from the NHL's first game, and right after another scintillating Patriots-Jets Monday Night Football thriller (the game was over before it started).

October is the promised land, nirvana, the pot of gold at the end of the rainbow for baseball fans. Every baseball fan wakes up on opening day wishing they can wake up 6 months later with their team still playing meaningful baseball, and those lucky fans in D.C. and Houston have not had their reveries interrupted yet. The World Series has always been the pinnacle of championships for me along with millions of other baseball fans. Always has been...but will it always be?

The Astros have made no light of the fact that they pride themselves on being one of the most analytically-forward teams in all of sports, not just baseball. It has spawned accusations of impropriety on the field - Trevor "Tyler" Bauer calling into question the methodology and research behind the Astros' spin rate revolution come to mind. The issues spill off the field too, but the Astros have won so much since 2015 that there is only so much rent in the public's brain for Astros-related opinions; to be quite frank, wondering about Zach Greinke's 50-word press conferences or what Kate Upton is up to with her baseball husband (who's name escapes me) is much quirkier, much more easily digestible, and less controversial than their oft-criticized trade in 2018 to bring in Roberto Osuna, alleged domestic violence abuser.

I will not pretend to be the preeminent voice on domestic violence in baseball and what repercussions teams should face for knowingly employing men who have been accused of these crimes. My favorite team would probably not have won a World Series without the contributions of a man who was suspended by Major League Baseball for an incident he had with his partner. I cannot say that decency should always stand above winning when the team I support did not take that stand and I continue to support them.

I didn't feel great when the Cubs traded for Chapman, and I do not feel great when I hear comments that seem to exonerate these men with time. I did not feel great when I read the story that an Astros executive allegedly (and corroborated by multiple reporters, including Sports Illustrated) screamed at a group of female reporters about the pleasure he took and has continued to take in the acquisition of the closer who almost blew the game not an hour earlier. The outburst was laden with profanity, misogyny, and terror, and plainly sounded terrifying.

I do not feel great that the Astros are hoping to sweep this story under the rug by discrediting the reporter in question, by gaslighting everyone in the locker room by simply saying the events didn't happen the way they did, by implying the reporter was making something out of nothing, and by straight-up disenfranchising scores of women around the sport of baseball by saying, "We won - so what?". This seems to be the preferred method of baseball executives - win a little and history will soon forget the transgressions of the past. This is no more apparent than when Hal Steinbrenner commented on acquiring Aroldis Chapman (on the losing side of Saturday's pennant-clincher, no less) that "he paid the penalty. Sooner or later, we forget, right?"

I try to follow a balance of men and women as they report on baseball. I feel lucky to subscribe to the Athletic, which in my personal opinion gives a preferred platform to women on the beat for multiple teams in baseball. Through the Athletic, I've been able to expand my coterie of Twitter follows, and the consistent theme across baseball coverage today is that people, men and women alike, are disappointed, but not surprised that this happened. They are not surprised (in my opinion) because it has happened before, it will happen again this year, and it will continue to happen in the future.

I do not know how to write about this so excuse me if my wording is a little clumsy at times. It just disappoints me. A lot of things right now in the world disappoint me, particularly this new breed of brazen, emboldened, unapologetic, "sorry-we're-not-sorry-what-are-you-going-to-do-about-it" hyper-masculine approach to achieving success at the expense of basic human decency. Gaslighting is de rigeur these days it seems for successful corporations. If you double down on accusing the accusers without giving in or admitting guilt, someone (or something) is going to have to blink, and the Astros show no signs of closing their eyes. The problem is, even with their eyes wide open, they can't seem to observe the repercussions of their choices and how they will reverberate throughout baseball.

It is a damn shame that these stories show no signs of ebbing in the near future, because if we focus on the Fall Classic from a pure on-field perspective, this World Series has the potential to be one of the best starting pitching matchups in recent history. Add in a first-time championship participant, a team that's hungry to cement itself as baseball's next dynasty, MVP candidates and redemption stories in both dugouts, and two (until now) nationally liked teams, and this is shaping up to be quite an entertaining match up. The Nationals have quietly, or as quietly as a World Series contender can get, been one of the best 3 teams in baseball since late May - they certainly surprised me.

I know I won't be the only one with the Taubman/Osuna story on my mind as Cole and Scherzer are dealing tonight, but the difference between me and baseball's female fans is I dont have to worry about something like that ever happening to me, where I feel vehemently uncomfortable in my place of work. The female reporters who have to keep covering the sport through the conclusion of the World Series will certainly have to think about it, be incessantly reminded of it every time they step in the locker room, and add it to their long list of workplace offenses.

As much as this story offends me, I cannot begin to imagine the depths of pain and betrayal any female reporters, employees, or fans of the Astros and MLB are feeling currently. I can only write about my thoughts, empathize, and try to understand what I can do myself in order to help and be an ally in any way I can.

At what length will teams go to in order to win? This is different than MLB juicing (and un-juicing) baseballs. At my core, I like to believe we are all decent people, even when competing for valor and hardware so bloodthirstily. The World Series should not be a referendum on ethics, even though sports and social issues have become more intertwined with the boom of social media and proliferation of independent thought over those platforms. The World Series should be the pinnacle of team achievement in baseball.

It is tremendously tone-deaf and self-defeating to disregard a key demographic, extensive evidence and corroboration of harassment, and basic human decency as the Astros have done. What was a wholly likable team has now shown repeatedly it does not care about public perception as long as they win. Acquiring Osuna was and could have been explained in baseball-only terms - and while I personally disagree about acquiring scumbags, that argument held weight as a one-off purchase of a distressed asset. Now that the Astros have shown again that personal conduct and being an upstanding man off-the-field doesn't matter as long as you win, we have come full circle. Osuna is not an exception, a market inefficiency that the Astros took a one-time advantage of - this is a trend that is emblematic of the culture they have (willingly and knowingly) built and allowed to succeed. The Astros are hailed as one of the smartest teams in baseball - time to pull the wool up from over your eyes and act like it, Houston. Taubman should not see the inside of Minute Maid Park during the World Series.

Nationals in 7.
